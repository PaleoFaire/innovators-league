# The Innovators League 30 — 2026

*Thirty companies. We stood in every factory.*

Every year we meet hundreds of founders — in their factories, on their test stands, on their firing ranges. The Innovators League 30 is the shortlist that survives: the thirty companies we believe are actually inventing the future, chosen from the 989 we track and the hundreds of founders we've met.

The bar is simple. The problem has to matter at civilizational scale. The technology has to be real — hardware you can stand next to, not a pitch deck. And we have to have seen it ourselves. This is not a poll and not a press-release digest: we walked these factory floors.

The list is unranked; companies appear alphabetically within five theses. Our fund holds positions in several of these companies — we mark each one, because conviction with skin in the game is the point.

---

## I. The Arsenal Gets Rebuilt

*The cheap, precise, mass-produced weapon is beating the exquisite one — and America's arsenal is being rebuilt by twenty-something founders in El Segundo and Austin, not by the primes.*

### 1. AndrenaM

*AI ears for the ocean — a sonar mesh that hears what satellites can't see.*

**Founders** Matej Cernosek, Alex Chu · **HQ** Hawthorne, CA · **Stage** Seed · **Backers** First Round Capital, Also Capital, Long Journey

**First-hand** — Met co-founder Matej Cernosek in Hawthorne this July; first interviewed him in April 2026.

The ocean is the last place on Earth we still can't really see into. AndrenaM is building ears for it: a mesh of underwater microphones that listen with onboard AI for the sound-signature of a submarine, a sea drone, or a ship running silent with its transponder off. Founded by ex-SpaceX and Palantir engineers who raised their first $10 million in 36 hours, it's turning the sea's blind spots into a live map for the U.S. Navy.

### 2. Chariot Defense

*The power plant in a box that lets laser weapons fire.*

**Founder** Adam Warmoth · **HQ** San Bruno, CA · **Stage** Series A · **Backers** a16z, General Catalyst, Shield Capital

**First-hand** — Toured the San Bruno factory with founder Adam Warmoth, July 9, 2026.

Modern armies run on electricity — drones, radios, jammers, and now lasers — but still haul it around as diesel, in convoys that get soldiers killed. Chariot builds rugged battery-microgrid units, called Amphora, that replace the generators and deliver the sudden surge of power a laser needs to knock a drone out of the sky. Warmoth's team has already replaced three diesel generators with one box at an Army exercise. The kilowatt-hour, not the gallon, is becoming the unit that wins fights.

### 3. CX2

*Shoot the archer, not the arrow — electronic warfare at drone-war prices.*

**Founders** Nathan Mintz, Porter Smith · **HQ** El Segundo, CA · **Stage** Series A · **Backers** Point72, a16z, 8VC

**First-hand** — Met founder Nathan Mintz in El Segundo this July.

Today's battlefields are drowning in invisible signals — jammers, GPS spoofers, drone-control links — and the side that masters them wins. CX2 builds gear that hunts the enemy by the radio waves they can't hide, pinpoints the source, and helps destroy it. Its drones and clip-on payloads can turn a $500 quadcopter into a hunter that finds the operator behind a whole swarm. Mintz has built electronic-warfare companies twice before; this one is already flying in Army exercises without contractor support.

### 4. Fortastra

*Bodyguard satellites for the 12,000 spacecraft that can't dodge.*

**Founder** Mike Smayda · **HQ** Torrance, CA · **Stage** Seed · **Backers** Upfront Ventures, Generational Partners, Bloomberg Beta

**First-hand** — Met founder Mike Smayda in Torrance this July.

Nearly every working satellite in orbit was built assuming nobody would ever attack it — and most can't even move out of the way. Fortastra builds the bodyguard: a nimble escort craft that can approach, inspect, shadow, and if needed physically defend another spacecraft in a contested orbit. As space quietly becomes a theater of war, Smayda — a SpaceX veteran who co-founded hypersonics maker Hermeus — is early to a category the primes will soon need and don't have.

### 5. Galadyne

*Long-range missiles built like cars, not like artisanal explosives.*

**Founder** Chandler Luzsicza · **HQ** Austin, TX · **Stage** Pre-Seed · **Backers** a16z American Dynamism, Pax Ventures

**First-hand** — Walked the Austin factory with founder Chandler Luzsicza, July 7, 2026.

America would run low on long-range missiles within roughly a week of serious fighting, because nearly every solid rocket motor traces back to a single chemical plant in Utah. Galadyne deletes the chokepoint by going liquid — kerosene and high-test peroxide, non-toxic and storable — so missiles can roll off an ordinary factory line with no explosives on it, and get fueled in the field. Luzsicza is 24, ex-SpaceX propulsion, and building toward missiles by the tens of thousands.

### 6. Neros

*$2,000 drones that kill tanks — made in America by the million.*

**Founders** Soren Monroe-Anderson, Olaf Hichwa · **HQ** El Segundo, CA · **Stage** Series B · **Backers** Sequoia, Founders Fund

**First-hand** — Interviewed co-founder Olaf Hichwa in El Segundo, November 2025.

In Ukraine, a drone cheaper than a steakhouse dinner routinely destroys a multi-million-dollar tank. Neros builds those drones in America: small, jam-resistant FPV attack aircraft, shipping thousands a year to the front, with a factory designed to make up to a million a year. The thesis is being proven in combat right now — the future of war belongs to whoever manufactures precision at consumer scale and consumer prices. Right now, that's a team of ex-drone-racers in El Segundo.

### 7. Picogrid

*One language for every machine on the battlefield.*

**Founders** Zane Mountcastle, Martin Slosarik · **HQ** El Segundo, CA · **Stage** Series A · **Backers** Initialized Capital, Starburst

**First-hand** — Interviewed CEO Zane Mountcastle.

In Ukraine, soldiers hand-wire $300 drones to $50,000 jammers to $5 million radars because none of them speak the same language. Picogrid is the translator: software and rugged field boxes that let every sensor, camera, and weapon plug in and fight as one system. It's the connective tissue the big primes never had an incentive to build — and it's already under Army contract, quietly becoming the layer everything else on this list will plug into.

### 8. Pilgrim

*A smoke detector for biological threats — making the invisible enemy visible.*

**Founder** Jake Adler · **HQ** San Francisco, CA · **Stage** Seed · **Backers** Cantos, Thiel Capital, Conviction

**First-hand** — Sat down with founder Jake Adler in San Francisco. *The ROS fund is an investor.*

Every threat humanity has beaten, we first learned to see: radar for bombers, smoke alarms for fires. Biology is still invisible — in June 2026, a false anthrax alarm locked down part of the Pentagon, because today's detectors can't tell a bioweapon from harmless soil bacteria. Pilgrim's ARGUS is the missing instrument: sensors that sample the air and are built to name the pathogen in minutes, not days. Adler — who once tested his own clotting bandage by drilling holes in his thighs — wants biology watched the way radar watches the sky.

## II. The Power Problem

*AI has turned electricity into the scarcest commodity in America. Gas turbines are sold out into the 2030s — these companies are building the power that fills the gap.*

### 9. Antares

*Micro nuclear reactors that roll off a factory line and onto a truck.*

**Founders** Jordan Bramble, Julia DeWahl · **HQ** Torrance, CA · **Stage** Series B · **Backers** Shine Capital, Alt Capital, Caffeinated

**First-hand** — Toured the Torrance factory with CEO Jordan Bramble, July 2, 2026.

A nuclear reactor the size of a shipping container that you truck to where the power is needed and switch on. Antares builds small heat-pipe reactors designed to be mass-produced like engines, not poured in concrete over a decade. In June 2026 its Mark-0 became the first privately built reactor to reach criticality under the government's pilot program — the first new reactor to go live at Idaho National Lab in over fifty years. Standing on their factory floor, it looks less like a power plant and more like a production line. That's the point.

### 10. Standard Electron

*Power as a product: solar-and-battery units that ship like appliances.*

**Founders** Tyler Norwood, Alex Le Roux · **HQ** Austin, TX · **Stage** Seed · **Backers** Antler, Wave Function, Earl Grey

**First-hand** — Walked the 35,000 sq ft Austin factory with CEO Tyler Norwood, July 7, 2026.

A factory or oilfield that needs more power today faces a brutal choice: wait years for the grid, or run diesel around the clock. Standard Electron sells electricity like an appliance — factory-built solar-plus-battery units that arrive as a finished product, not a construction project. Norwood ran Antler's US business before jumping to the other side of the table; co-founder Alex Le Roux built ICON's house-printing robots. Their bet: the fastest way to add power to America is to manufacture it.

### 11. Terraform Industries

*Natural gas made from sunlight and air.*

**Founder** Casey Handmer · **HQ** Burbank, CA · **Stage** Series A · **Backers** Khosla Ventures, ReGen, Collaborative Fund

**First-hand** — Visited the Mojave test site and Burbank HQ with founder Casey Handmer, July 8, 2026 — and interviewed him on the ROS podcast.

An oil well pulls fuel from the ground; Terraform pulls it from thin air. Its plant — small enough to fit in two shipping containers — captures CO₂ from the atmosphere, splits hydrogen from water, and combines them into pipeline-grade natural gas, powered entirely by cheap solar. Now that solar has fallen ~99% in cost, the math finally works. Handmer, a physicist who quit NASA's Jet Propulsion Lab for this, calls it what it is: a synthetic oil well running on sunshine.

### 12. Valar Atomics

*Nuclear "gigasites": hundreds of small reactors, mass-produced to feed AI.*

**Founder** Isaiah Taylor · **HQ** Hawthorne, CA · **Stage** Series B · **Backers** Riot Ventures, Snowpoint, Palmer Luckey

**First-hand** — Met founder Isaiah Taylor; ranked #1 in our SMR Power Rankings.

The AI boom is hitting a wall: there isn't enough electricity. Valar's answer is to mass-produce it — "gigasites" packed with hundreds of small, stripped-down reactors that together generate gigawatts for AI data centers. Its first test reactor, Ward 250, went critical in Utah in June 2026, and Taylor's pitch is pure manufacturing heresy: reactor physics is solved; the bottleneck is factory cost. Backed by Anduril's Palmer Luckey, Valar is treating the atom like a product line.

## III. America Makes Things Again

*The machines, factories, and homes America forgot how to build — being rebuilt by teams who treat manufacturing itself as the product.*

### 13. American Housing Corporation

*Rowhouses built like furniture, shipped in containers.*

**Founders** Bobby Fijan, Riley Meik · **HQ** Austin, TX · **Stage** Pre-Seed · **Backers** Antler, Flux Capital, New Founding

**First-hand** — Met co-founder Bobby Fijan in Austin.

America takes seven months and a small army of subcontractors to build a single house. American Housing builds them like furniture instead: an Austin factory produces a kit-of-parts rowhouse — walls, floors, stairs — engineered to fold into standard shipping containers and snap together on site. The goal is blunt and overdue: make the starter home buildable again in the cities where young families have been priced out.

### 14. Bedrock Robotics

*Self-driving excavators — a brain bolted onto the machines crews already own.*

**Founders** Boris Sofman, Kevin Peterson · **HQ** San Francisco, CA · **Stage** Series B · **Backers** CapitalG, Eclipse, 8VC

**First-hand** — Met founder Boris Sofman in Austin this July.

Construction is short nearly a million workers, and the machines sit idle half the time. Bedrock doesn't build new robots — it bolts a reversible kit of cameras, sensors, and computers onto the excavators and dozers crews already own, so a standard digger works the site with nobody in the cab. Sofman taught Waymo's trucks to drive highways; his machines have been digging on live sites since 2025, and the company hit a $1.75 billion valuation in under two years.

### 15. Cuby Technologies

*A portable factory that turns out houses in 30 days.*

**Founders** Aleks Gampel, Aleh Kandrashou · **HQ** Austin, TX · **Stage** Series B · **Backers** Vannevar Ventures, At One Ventures

**First-hand** — Interviewed co-founder Aleks Gampel, November 2025.

Instead of building a house on a lot, Cuby ships a factory to the neighborhood: an entire home-production line packed into ~122 shipping containers, trucked to site, and run under a pressurized dome — turning out finished houses in about 30 days versus America's typical seven months. The real trick is that the factory itself is copy-pasteable. Housing stops being a construction problem and becomes a replication problem.

### 16. Durin

*Drill rigs that run themselves — dragging mineral exploration out of the 1950s.*

**Founder** Ted Feldmann · **HQ** Los Angeles, CA · **Stage** Pre-Seed · **Backers** 8090 Industries, Lux Capital, 1517 Fund

**First-hand** — Four sit-downs with founder Ted Feldmann.

The world needs vastly more copper and lithium to electrify — but the drills that find them still work like it's 1955: run by hand, one hole at a time. Durin builds diamond-tipped rigs that drill around the clock on their own, logging every vibration and rotation as data. Faster, cheaper, tireless exploration for the metals the entire modern economy is about to run short of — mining's oldest job, finally joining the software age.

### 17. Rangeview

*A robot foundry that casts superalloy parts in 12 days, not 6 months.*

**Founders** Cameron Schiller, Aeden Gasser-Brennan · **HQ** El Segundo, CA · **Stage** Seed · **Backers** Calibrate, Shield Capital, TenOneTen

**First-hand** — Two factory visits (April 2025, July 2026) with founder Cameron Schiller — in the building where Space Shuttle engines were tested.

America has lost more than a hundred foundries since 2001, and the survivors quote six months and $300,000 in tooling for one complex casting. Rangeview 3D-prints the ceramic mold straight from a design file and pours superalloy at 1,500°C — twelve days, door to door. The demand signal is the story: Schiller turns away roughly ten times the revenue he accepts. The constraint on American reindustrialization isn't orders. It's capacity.

### 18. Senra Systems

*The hand-built wiring inside every jet and rocket — automated at last.*

**Founders** Jordan Black, Ben Shanahan · **HQ** Los Angeles, CA · **Stage** Series B · **Backers** General Catalyst, CIV, Dylan Field

**First-hand** — Met founder Jordan Black. *The ROS fund invested in the $65M Series B.*

Every fighter jet, satellite, and robot is held together by miles of wiring harnesses — still built by hand, still the slowest and most error-prone part of any advanced machine. Senra's software turns a wiring diagram into a finished harness, so a new hire builds in hours what once took a year to learn. It ships roughly a thousand harnesses a month, many into SpaceX rockets, and closed a $65 million Series B in July 2026.

### 19. The Boring Company

*Tunnels at 1% of the old price.*

**Founder** Elon Musk · **HQ** Bastrop, TX · **Stage** Series G · **Backers** Vy Capital, Sequoia, Valor

**First-hand** — Toured the Bastrop factory, July 7, 2026.

Digging a tunnel under a city normally costs around a billion dollars a mile — largely because the boring machine is used once and abandoned underground. Boring Company machines crawl on to the next mile instead, cutting the cost toward $12–15 million. At Bastrop we watched Prufrock segments come off the line like auto parts. The vision: out-dig the entire rest of the country, every year, and make the space under cities usable again.

## IV. The Sky Is Open for Business

*Launch got cheap. Now the second-order companies — factories in orbit, mines on asteroids, engines past Mach 3 — get to exist.*

### 20. Apex Space

*The mass-produced satellite bus — the Dell of space.*

**Founders** Ian Cinnamon, Max Benassi · **HQ** Los Angeles, CA · **Stage** Series E · **Backers** a16z, 8VC, Point72

**First-hand** — Met CEO Ian Cinnamon at Factory One in LA — twice.

Every satellite needs a body: the chassis that holds the solar panels, thrusters, and electronics. That bus used to take years and tens of millions, built one at a time. Apex mass-produces it — first one shipped in twelve months, and the line at Factory One now runs like a car plant. $718 million raised at a $2.3 billion valuation says the market agrees: the coming swarm of thousands of satellites needs someone selling standardized bodies.

### 21. Astro Mechanica

*A jet engine with gears — efficient from runway to past the speed of sound.*

**Founder** Ian Brooke · **HQ** San Francisco, CA · **Stage** Series A · **Backers** Pioneer Fund, a16z

**ROS coverage** — Featured in our supersonic deep dive, "Dude, Where's My Supersonic Jet?"

A jet engine, like a car stuck in one gear, is only efficient in a narrow band of speeds. Astro Mechanica gives it a gearbox: the turbine and compressor are split apart with an electric motor between them, so the engine runs efficiently from a standing start to supersonic. The prize is an aircraft cheap to fly slow and able to fly fast — reopening the door the Concorde slammed shut fifty years ago.

### 22. AstroForge

*Mining platinum from asteroids with probes cheaper than a house in LA.*

**Founders** Matt Gialich, Jose Acain · **HQ** Huntington Beach, CA · **Stage** Series A · **Backers** Nova Threshold, 776, Initialized

**First-hand** — Interviewed CEO Matt Gialich for the ROS podcast.

A single metal-rich asteroid can hold more platinum than humanity has ever mined. AstroForge builds cheap robotic probes to go get it — its Odin spacecraft already flew deeper into space than any private company in history. Gialich's insight is that failure is affordable: build probes so cheap you can lose several and still win. If it works, the most valuable materials on Earth turn out to be waiting a few million miles overhead.

### 23. Hermeus

*Chasing the SR-71's record — the fastest air-breathing plane since 1976.*

**Founders** AJ Piplica, Skyler Shuford · **HQ** Atlanta, GA · **Stage** Series C · **Backers** Khosla, Founders Fund, In-Q-Tel

**First-hand** — Interviewed CEO AJ Piplica, November 2025.

The fastest jet ever built retired in the 1990s, and nothing has matched it since. Hermeus is trying: its Chimera engine takes off as an ordinary jet, then transforms mid-flight into a ramjet past Mach 3 — the transition that has stumped engineers for decades. Defense aircraft come first; the endgame is New York to Tokyo in about ninety minutes. A billion-dollar valuation says this is no longer a paper airplane.

### 24. Varda Space Industries

*Drug factories in orbit — medicine that can only be made without gravity.*

**Founders** Will Bruey, Delian Asparouhov · **HQ** El Segundo, CA · **Stage** Series C · **Backers** Khosla, Founders Fund

**ROS coverage** — A 2026 Rational Optimist Award winner.

In zero gravity, drug crystals form more perfectly than any lab on Earth can grow them — and the shape of a crystal can decide whether a medicine works. Varda builds capsules that manufacture pharmaceuticals in orbit and parachute them home; it has already flown and recovered multiple missions. Multibillion-dollar drugmaker United Therapeutics is paying for flights. Space is quietly becoming a factory floor for the things gravity won't allow.

## V. Taming the Physical World

*Oceans, skies, deserts, even the sense of smell — the hard, unglamorous frontiers where atoms still refuse to obey software.*

### 25. Airship Industries

*Cargo blimps bigger than the Hindenburg that need no port, no runway, no crew.*

**Founder** James Coutre · **HQ** Los Angeles, CA · **Stage** Seed · **Backers** Eli Dourado's syndicate, Not Boring, Wave Function

**First-hand** — Visited twice (Nov 2025, Jul 2026). I pressed their 140-lb airship frame over my head — it's engineered to lift 40,000 lbs.

Picture a cargo blimp longer than three football fields lowering a shipping container into an open field on a winch. That's what this team of ex-SpaceX and Hyperloop engineers is building: self-flying airships that move ocean freight at air-freight speed for a fraction of the cost and fuel. A plane crosses the Pacific in a day, but real door-to-door freight takes a week. An airship that skips the ports skips the week.

### 26. Osmo

*Giving computers a sense of smell — the MP3 moment for the last analog sense.*

**Founder** Alex Wiltschko · **HQ** New York, NY · **Stage** Series B · **Backers** Lux Capital, GV, Two Sigma

**First-hand** — Visited Osmo's labs, November 2025.

We digitized sight and sound decades ago — never smell. Osmo captures a scent as data in one lab and rebuilds it from raw chemicals in another. Beyond new perfumes and flavors, the deeper prize is medical: machines that smell could one day catch Parkinson's and certain cancers on a person's breath, years before other symptoms. Wiltschko spent a decade teaching machines olfaction at Google Brain; now the nose is becoming an instrument.

### 27. Poseidon

*Self-flying cargo craft that skim the waves — faster than ships, cheaper than planes.*

**Founders** David Zagaynov, Parker Tenney · **HQ** San Francisco, CA · **Stage** Seed · **Backers** Tamarack Global, Draper Associates

**First-hand** — Met co-founder David Zagaynov in San Francisco this July.

Between the boat and the plane sits a forgotten machine: the ekranoplan, which flies a few feet above the water on a cushion of trapped air. Poseidon is reviving it with modern composites and autonomy — pilotless craft hauling roughly two tons to coastlines and islands that have no airport or harbor. Zagaynov calls it "a cheap F-150 for the sky." An entire layer of transport the world abandoned decades ago is coming back, without the pilot.

### 28. Ulysses

*Untethered robots that guard the seabed — and replant it.*

**Founders** Akhil Voorakkara, Jamie Wedderburn · **HQ** San Francisco, CA · **Stage** Series A · **Backers** a16z, Booz Allen Ventures, Lowercarbon

**ROS network** — We introduced Ulysses to Abu Dhabi's sovereign ecosystem.

Guarding the undersea cables that carry the internet normally requires a crewed ship and a robot on a leash. Ulysses cuts both: its Mako robots swim down alone — no ship, no tether — patrolling seabed infrastructure and hunting mines for defense customers. The same machines replant seagrass meadows that capture carbon faster than rainforest. Robots that both protect and heal the deep ocean, backed by a16z's defense practice.

### 29. Vital Lyfe

*Clean drinking water from any source, anywhere — desalination in a box.*

**Founders** Jonathan Criss, Andrew Harner · **HQ** Torrance, CA · **Stage** Seed · **Backers** Interlagos, General Catalyst

**First-hand** — Met founder Jonathan Criss in Torrance this July.

Two billion people lack reliably clean water, and today's fix — a giant desalination plant — is expensive and immovable. Vital Lyfe shrinks it into a portable box: advanced membranes and smart power that turn seawater, river water, even a puddle into drinking water, no infrastructure required. Criss and Harner built hardware for SpaceX's Starlink and Dragon; their first product, Access, is shipping this year. A machine that used to fill a building, down to something you can carry.

### 30. Zipline

*The largest autonomous delivery network on Earth — it started with blood.*

**Founders** Keller Rinaudo Cliffton, Keenan Wyrobek · **HQ** South San Francisco, CA · **Stage** Series G · **Backers** Sequoia, a16z, Baillie Gifford

**ROS network** — We introduced Zipline to Abu Dhabi's sovereign investors.

Zipline's drones deliver about 70% of Rwanda's blood supply, parachuting it to remote clinics in minutes — and in Texas they lower Walmart orders onto suburban lawns in under thirty minutes. Over 125 million autonomous miles flown, more than the rest of the industry combined, with zero public-safety incidents. Where Zipline operates, mothers stop dying in childbirth for lack of blood. The drone-delivery future isn't coming. In two countries, it's routine.
