# The Innovators League 30

*The thirty companies we believe are actually inventing the future.*

Every year we meet hundreds of founders — in their factories, on their test stands, with our own eyes and hands. The Innovators League 30 is the shortlist that survives: the thirty companies we think are genuinely building the world that comes next, not iterating on the one we already have.

It's an exclusive club by design, and the bar to make it is high. A company has to be attacking a problem that matters at a civilizational scale. The technology has to be *real* — hardware you can stand next to, not a pitch deck. And the team has to be pulling the future forward rather than optimizing the past. Thirty spots. No more.

Here they are, in alphabetical order.

---

**1. Airship Industries** — Picture a cargo blimp longer than three football fields drifting silently over an open field and lowering a shipping container to the ground on a winch — no port, no runway, no crew. That's what a team of ex-SpaceX engineers is building: giant, self-flying airships that carry freight across oceans at the speed of air cargo for a fraction of the cost and fuel. The insight is simple. A plane crosses the Pacific in a day, but real freight takes a week once you add trucks, warehouses, and customs. An airship that picks up and drops off directly skips all of it.

**2. American Housing Corporation** — America takes seven months and a small army of subcontractors to build a single house. American Housing builds them like furniture instead. Its Austin factory produces a "kit of parts" for a rowhouse — walls, floors, stairs — engineered to fold into standard shipping containers, trucked to the lot, and snapped together on site in a fraction of the time. The goal is blunt and overdue: make the starter home buildable again in the expensive cities where young families have been priced out.

**3. AndrenaM** — The ocean is the last place on Earth we still can't really see into. AndrenaM is building ears for it: a mesh of cheap underwater microphones that drift in the water and, with onboard AI, listen for the distinct sound-signature of a submarine, a sea drone, or a ship — even one running silent with its transponder switched off. Founded by ex-SpaceX, Palantir, and Anduril engineers (who raised their first $10M in about 36 hours), it turns the sea's blind spots into a live map for the U.S. Navy.

**4. Antares** — A nuclear reactor the size of a shipping container that you can truck to wherever power is needed and switch on. Antares builds small "heat-pipe" reactors designed to roll off a factory line like engines, not be poured in concrete over a decade. In 2026 its first unit became the first privately built reactor in America to reach criticality under the government's pilot program — the first new reactor to go live at Idaho National Lab in over fifty years. Clean, firm power, mass-produced and portable.

**5. Apex Space** — Every satellite needs a body — the chassis that holds the solar panels, thrusters, and electronics together. Historically that "bus" took years and tens of millions to build, one at a time. Apex mass-produces it, shipping its first in twelve months for a fraction of the old price and cranking out more like a car plant. It's the Dell of space: not the flashy payload, but the standardized machine everything else bolts onto — and the reason the coming swarm of thousands of satellites can actually get built.

**6. Astro Mechanica** — A jet engine, like a car, is only efficient in a narrow band of speeds. Astro Mechanica gives it "gears." It splits the engine's turbine and compressor apart and slips an electric motor between them, so the engine can run efficiently from a standing start on the runway all the way past the speed of sound. The prize is an aircraft that's cheap to fly slow and able to fly supersonic — reopening a door the Concorde slammed shut fifty years ago.

**7. AstroForge** — A single metal-rich asteroid can hold more platinum than humanity has ever mined. AstroForge builds cheap robotic probes to go get it — flying out to near-Earth asteroids and using onboard tools to break the ore free and refine the precious metals in space. Its Odin probe already flew farther into deep space than any private company before it. If it works, the most valuable materials on Earth turn out to be sitting, unclaimed, a few million miles above our heads.

**8. Bedrock Robotics** — There's a shortage of nearly a million construction workers, and the machines already sit idle half the time. Bedrock's answer isn't to build new robots — it's to bolt a "brain" onto the excavators and bulldozers crews already own. A reversible kit of cameras, sensors, and computers lets a standard digger work an entire job site by itself, nobody in the cab. Founded by the ex-Waymo engineer who taught trucks to drive highways, it learns the job by doing it, not by being programmed.

**9. Chariot Defense** — Modern armies run on electricity — drones, radios, sensors, and now laser weapons — but they still get it from diesel generators and truckloads of fuel that get soldiers killed on resupply runs. Chariot builds the power plant in a box: rugged battery-and-microgrid units, called Amphora, that replace the generators and deliver the sudden bursts of power a laser needs to knock a drone out of the sky. Founded by an Archer alum, it's trying to make the kilowatt-hour, not the gallon, the unit that wins the fight.

**10. Cuby Technologies** — Instead of building a house on a lot, Cuby ships a factory to the neighborhood. It packs an entire home-production line into roughly 122 shipping containers, trucks it to the site, inflates a pressurized dome over it, and starts turning out finished houses in about 30 days each — versus America's typical seven months. The real trick is that the factory itself is copy-pasteable: once one works, you can drop the same line anywhere. Housing stops being a construction problem and becomes a replication problem.

**11. CX2** — Today's battlefields are drowning in invisible signals — jammers, GPS spoofers, drone-control links — and the side that masters them wins. CX2 builds electronic-warfare gear that hunts the enemy by the radio waves they can't hide, pinpoints the source, and helps destroy it: "shoot the archer, not the arrow." Its drones and clip-on payloads can turn a $500 quadcopter into a tool that finds and kills the operator behind a whole swarm. Founded by a defense veteran who's done this before, it's building the electronic-warfare layer the slow-moving primes can't.

**12. Durin** — The world needs vastly more copper, lithium, and rare metals to electrify — but the drills that find them still work the way they did in the 1950s, run by hand, one hole at a time. Durin builds diamond-tipped drilling rigs that run themselves around the clock, logging every vibration and rotation as data. Faster, cheaper, tireless exploration means finding the critical metals the entire modern economy is about to run short of — and dragging the oldest part of mining into the software age.

**13. Fortastra** — Nearly all of the 12,000 working satellites in orbit were built assuming no one would ever come after them — and most can't even dodge. Fortastra builds their bodyguard: a nimble "escort" satellite that can sneak up on, inspect, shadow, and if needed physically defend another spacecraft in a contested orbit. As space turns into contested territory, it's the difference between a satellite that's a sitting duck and one that has backup. Founded by a SpaceX and Hermeus veteran.

**14. Galadyne** — America would run low on long-range missiles in about a week of serious fighting, because nearly every solid-fuel motor traces back to a single chemical plant in Utah. Galadyne sidesteps the chokepoint entirely by going liquid — burning kerosene and high-test peroxide instead of packed solid propellant — so missiles can be built on an ordinary factory line, with the fuel added in the field. Led by a 24-year-old ex-SpaceX propulsion engineer, the goal is to make long-range missiles the way Detroit makes cars: by the tens of thousands.

**15. Hermeus** — The fastest jet ever built, the SR-71 Blackbird, was retired in the 1990s, and nothing has matched its air-breathing speed since. Hermeus is trying to beat it. Its "Chimera" engine starts as an ordinary jet, then transforms in flight into a ramjet once it passes Mach 3 — the hard part that's stumped engineers for decades. First it means aircraft for defense; eventually it means a passenger flight from New York to Tokyo in about 90 minutes. It's a bet that the age of supersonic flight isn't over — it's just starting again.

**16. Neros** — In Ukraine, a $2,000 drone routinely destroys a multi-million-dollar tank — the cheap weapon has quietly become the decisive one. Neros builds those drones in America: small, jam-resistant FPV attack drones, each cheaper than a nice dinner, and it's already shipping thousands a year to the front. Its new factory is designed to make up to a million a year. The thesis is stark and being proven on the battlefield right now: the future of war belongs to whoever can manufacture precision at consumer scale and consumer prices.

**17. Osmo** — We digitized sight and sound decades ago, but never smell — the one sense a computer still can't record or replay. Osmo is changing that. It captures a scent as data in one lab and rebuilds it from raw chemicals in another, essentially creating the MP3 for smell. Beyond designing new perfumes and flavors, the deeper prize is medical: machines that can smell might one day detect diseases like Parkinson's and certain cancers on a person's breath, years before any other symptom appears.

**18. Picogrid** — On the modern battlefield, a $300 drone, a $50,000 jammer, and a $5M radar usually can't talk to each other — soldiers wire them together by hand because none of them speak the same language. Picogrid is the translator: software and rugged little boxes that let every sensor, weapon, and camera plug in and work as one system. It's the connective tissue the big defense primes never had the incentive to build — turning a pile of incompatible gear into a single, coordinated picture of the fight.

**19. Pilgrim** — While the defense world pours billions into drones, Pilgrim is building the humble thing the drone can't do: stop a soldier from bleeding out in seconds. Its clay-packed gauze, Kingsfoil, clots a catastrophic wound almost instantly. And its 22-year-old founder, Jake Adler, proved he believed in it the hard way — by filming himself drilling holes in his own thighs to test it head-to-head against the Army's standard-issue dressing. It's a reminder that some of the most important frontier tech is also the simplest.

**20. Poseidon** — Somewhere between a boat and a plane sits a forgotten craft: the ekranoplan, which flies just a few feet above the water on a cushion of trapped air. Poseidon is reviving it with modern materials and autonomy. Its self-flying vehicles skim the waves — far faster than any ship, far cheaper than any plane, and needing no runway or harbor. They can haul cargo straight to coastlines and islands that have no airport at all, opening a whole layer of transport the world quietly abandoned decades ago.

**21. Rangeview** — America has lost more than a hundred metal foundries since 2001, and the ones left take six months and hundreds of thousands of dollars in tooling to cast a single complex part. Rangeview replaced that with a robot foundry. It 3D-prints the ceramic mold straight from a digital design and pours molten superalloy at 1,500°C — turning a six-month job into about twelve days. Founded by a pair of former robotics kids in El Segundo, it's quietly rebuilding America's ability to make the metal parts that rockets, jets, and reactors are made of.

**22. Senra Systems** — Every fighter jet, satellite, and robot is held together by miles of wiring harnesses — the bundles of cables that carry power and data — and they're still built by hand, making them the slowest and most error-prone part of any advanced machine. Senra automates them. Its software turns a wiring diagram into a finished harness, so a brand-new hire can build in hours what used to take the industry a year to train for. It already ships about a thousand a month, many of them into SpaceX rockets.

**23. Standard Electron** — Factories and oilfields that need more power today face a brutal choice: wait years for the grid to catch up, or run a diesel generator around the clock. Standard Electron sells power like an appliance instead — factory-built units of solar panels and batteries that arrive as a finished product, not a construction project, delivering clean on-site electricity from day one. Founded by a former venture-firm leader and a co-founder of the house-printing company ICON, it's betting the fastest way to add power is to manufacture it, not build it.

**24. Terraform Industries** — An oil well pulls fuel out of the ground; Terraform pulls it out of thin air. Its chemical plant — small enough to fit in two shipping containers — sucks carbon dioxide from the atmosphere, splits hydrogen out of water, and combines them into pipeline-grade natural gas, powered entirely by cheap solar electricity. Now that solar has fallen roughly 99% in cost, the economics finally work. It's essentially a synthetic oil well you could run in your backyard, making fuel from nothing but sunlight, air, and water.

**25. The Boring Company** — Digging a tunnel under a city normally costs around a billion dollars a mile, largely because the giant boring machine is used once and abandoned underground. The Boring Company redesigned the machine to crawl forward continuously and move on to the next mile, slashing the cost toward $12–15 million. The vision is a network of car tunnels beneath cities that lets you skip surface traffic entirely — and, eventually, a company that can dig faster than the entire rest of the country combined.

**26. Ulysses** — Guarding the undersea cables that carry the internet, or hunting sea mines, normally means an expensive crewed ship and a robot on a tether. Ulysses cuts both. Its autonomous underwater robots, called Mako, swim down and work on their own — no ship, no leash — patrolling critical seabed infrastructure for defense. The same machines can replant seagrass meadows, which pull carbon from the water faster than a rainforest. Backed by a16z's defense fund and Booz Allen, it's building robots that both protect and heal the deep ocean.

**27. Valar Atomics** — The AI boom is running into a wall: there isn't enough electricity to feed the data centers. Valar's answer is to mass-produce power. It's building toward "gigasites" — single locations packed with hundreds of small, stripped-down nuclear reactors that together generate gigawatts, enough to run an AI campus. Its first test reactor, Ward 250, went critical in Utah in 2026. Backed by Anduril's Palmer Luckey and Palantir's president, it's a bet that the way to power the future is lots of small reactors, made like products.

**28. Varda Space Industries** — In the weightlessness of orbit, chemicals crystallize more perfectly than they ever can under Earth's gravity — which matters enormously for drugs, where the shape of a crystal can decide whether a medicine works. Varda builds small capsules that manufacture pharmaceuticals in space and parachute them back to the ground. It's not science fiction: the multibillion-dollar drugmaker United Therapeutics is already paying Varda to fly its medicines to orbit. Varda is quietly turning space into a factory floor for the things that can only be made where there's no gravity.

**29. Vital Lyfe** — Roughly two billion people lack reliably clean drinking water, and today's fix — a massive desalination plant — is expensive and immovable. Vital Lyfe shrinks it into a portable box. Using advanced membranes and smart power management, its systems turn almost any water — seawater, a muddy river, even a puddle — into clean drinking water, anywhere, with no fixed infrastructure. Founded by former SpaceX engineers who worked on Starlink and Dragon, its first product, Access, brings a machine that used to fill a building down to something you can carry.

**30. Zipline** — Zipline flies the world's largest autonomous delivery network, and it started with the hardest possible cargo: blood. Its drones now deliver about 70% of Rwanda's blood supply, dropping it by parachute to remote clinics in minutes instead of hours — and in the U.S., they lower Walmart orders onto suburban lawns in under 30 minutes. Over 125 million autonomous miles flown, more than the rest of the industry combined, with zero public-safety incidents. Where it operates, mothers stop dying in childbirth for lack of blood. It's the drone-delivery future, already real.
